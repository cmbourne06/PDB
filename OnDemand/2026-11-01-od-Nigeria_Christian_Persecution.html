Topic 1 — National Security & Foreign Affairs

National Security & Foreign Affairs · On-Demand

Nigeria's anti-Christian terror campaign kills thousands annually with no effective government response

BLUF

Nigeria accounts for roughly 72% of Christians killed for their faith worldwide, with more than 3,000 documented deaths and 3,000 abductions in the first eight months of 2026 alone — attacks that continued through this week in Plateau, Kaduna, and Benue states. The Tinubu government denies that the violence is religiously motivated; the U.S., European Parliament, and UN have escalated international pressure, with the U.S. House voting in July to withhold all aid, yet attacks are unabated.

Key Facts

  • Open Doors' 2026 World Watch List recorded 3,490 Christians killed in Nigeria during its reporting period (Oct 2024–Sep 2025) — 72% of the global total of 4,849 faith-related killings — and rated Nigeria #7 worldwide, with a violence score of 100/100 (Open Doors International, primary document).
  • Intersociety documented 3,083 Christians killed and 3,193 abducted in Nigeria from January through August 2026, with July–August alone accounting for 437 deaths and 393 abductions — the deadliest two-month stretch recorded this year (Arise TV Nigeria, digital outlet).
  • This week: On 28 Oct, Fulani assailants killed Pastor Yahaya Kambasaya and kidnapped 20 congregants in Farin Dutse village, Kaduna state. On 31 Oct–1 Nov, nine Christians were killed across three attacks in Plateau state. On 1–2 Nov, six Christians were killed in Benue state (Worthy News, digital outlet; Christian Today, digital outlet).
  • Three overlapping groups drive the violence: armed Fulani militias (responsible for 55% of Christian deaths, per Open Doors), Boko Haram (6%), and ISWAP, the Islamic State affiliate (2%). Fulani militias kill Christians at a rate of 6.2 Christians per Muslim killed; some analysts assess ISWAP is funding Fulani operations in the Middle Belt (Spanish Ministry of Defence, primary document).
  • Twelve of Nigeria's northern states operate under Sharia law, creating a parallel legal system under which Christians face second-class status and conversion from Islam can draw capital punishment; violence in the northeast flows primarily from Boko Haram and ISWAP, while attacks in the Middle Belt (Plateau, Benue, Kaduna, Nasarawa) are predominantly Fulani militia raids on Christian farming villages (Open Doors, official statement).
  • In July 2026, the European Parliament adopted an emergency resolution 510–1 condemning the persecution following the Kawel village massacre in Plateau state; in June 2026, a UN Special Rapporteur on Freedom of Religion conducted an official visit to Nigeria — her formal report has not yet been published (European Parliament, P10_TA(2026)0268, primary document).
  • The U.S. House of Representatives voted in July 2026 to withhold 100% of U.S. assistance to Nigeria; the Senate has not yet acted. The FY26 appropriations act already withholds partial funding pending demonstrable Nigerian government action. The U.S. House Appropriations Committee delivered a joint report on Christian persecution to the White House in February 2026, recommending a bilateral security agreement, targeted sanctions, and visa restrictions (U.S. House Appropriations Committee, official statement).

Points of Dispute

Is the violence religiously motivated or primarily driven by resource competition? The Tinubu government, advocacy groups aligned with Nigerian Muslim constituencies, and some Western security analysts hold that the violence reflects a multi-causal crisis — farmer-herder conflict over land and water driven by desertification, general insecurity, and ethnic tension — with religious identity incidental rather than motivating (The Guardian Nigeria, newspaper). Nigeria's Information Minister called Trump's November 2026 warning "unwarranted" and based on "faulty data" (Worthy News, digital outlet). Open Doors, the Observatory for Religious Freedom in Africa, the Spanish Ministry of Defence, the U.S. Congress, and the European Parliament hold that the 6.2:1 Christian-to-Muslim kill ratio, the specific targeting of churches and clergy, and the timing of attacks around Christian holidays reflect deliberate religious targeting that cannot be explained by resource competition alone (Open Doors International, primary document; ECLJ, official statement). Open Doors' own senior researcher acknowledged in 2025 that "Christians are still targeted, but increasingly some Muslims are targeted by Fulani militants" — a data point both sides cite (AllAfrica / BBC, digital outlet).

Is the Nigerian government complicit or merely ineffective? Senator Ted Cruz stated in May 2026 that "Nigerian officials have been, unfortunately, complicit in facilitating these atrocities," and U.S. Representative Riley Moore alleged Tinubu government complicity and sought a CPC presidential directive (Gazette Nigeria, digital outlet). International Christian Concern released a May 2026 report alleging a $10 million Nigerian government effort to suppress international reporting on the crisis (Mission Network News, digital outlet). The Tinubu government has denied all such allegations; the $10 million cover-up claim and the complicity allegations have not been independently corroborated in open-source reporting as of this edition.

Have U.S. actions produced results? President Trump claimed in June 2026 that previous U.S. military strikes had "largely ended the slaughter" of Nigerian Christians (ICIR Nigeria, digital outlet). Intersociety's casualty data for the same period shows no reduction: July–August 2026 were the deadliest months on record (Arise TV Nigeria, digital outlet). No independent reporting confirms a post-strike decline in attack rates.

Background

Nigeria's anti-Christian violence has three partially overlapping theaters. In the northeast (Borno, Adamawa, Yobe states), Boko Haram and ISWAP have operated since 2009, killing an estimated 40,000 people total across Nigeria, Niger, Cameroon, and Chad, with Christians killed at roughly 1.3–1.8 per Muslim. In the Middle Belt (Plateau, Benue, Kaduna, Nasarawa, Taraba states), armed Fulani militias began escalating attacks on Christian farming villages circa 2015; desertification has pushed Fulani herders south into traditionally Christian agricultural land, but the disproportionate targeting of Christian communities — not all farming communities — is the central evidence of religious motivation. In the north, Sharia law in 12 states has codified Christian second-class status since 2000. Nigeria ranked #12 on the Open Doors World Watch List in 2020; by 2026 it ranked #7 and scored the maximum violence rating. Over the 25 years from 2000 to 2025, Islamist movements in northern Nigeria are linked to more than 62,000 Christian deaths and the destruction of more than 1,800 churches — figures separate from the Boko Haram/ISWAP insurgency (Spanish Ministry of Defence, primary document). President Trump redesignated Nigeria a Country of Particular Concern on 31 Oct 2025; the Tinubu government rejected the designation and has since engaged in ongoing bilateral diplomacy aimed at preserving U.S. aid while resisting the religious-persecution framing.

Watch

  1. Will the U.S. Senate pass legislation withholding 100% of aid to Nigeria, and if so, will the Nigerian government respond with security action or diplomatic maneuver? Assessment: Roughly even chance the Senate acts before the end of the current legislative session (moderate confidence), based on Foreign Relations Committee alignment with the CPC designation and strong House vote; the primary obstacle is floor scheduling and a possible bilateral agreement between State and Abuja that gives the executive branch grounds to defer congressional action.
  2. Will the UN Special Rapporteur's formal report on her June 2026 Nigeria visit, when published, add enforceable pressure, or will it enter the body of unacted-upon documentation? Assessment: Unlikely to produce near-term enforcement (moderate confidence), based on the pattern of prior UN resolutions and European Parliament resolutions that have not produced behavioral change in Abuja, and the absence of a Security Council pathway that China and Russia would not block.
  3. Will attack rates in the Middle Belt decline, hold, or increase through the remainder of 2026 as international pressure intensifies? Assessment: Likely to hold or increase (low confidence), based on the acceleration of attacks through mid-year, the Nigerian military's demonstrated inability to interdict Fulani militia operations preemptively, and no reported change in the security posture in affected states; a decrease would require Nigerian military redeployment or a negotiated disengagement with Fulani leadership — neither of which has been signaled.
