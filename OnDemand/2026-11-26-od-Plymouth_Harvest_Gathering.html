Plymouth Settlers Host Three-Day Gathering With 90 Armed Wampanoag Delegation as Harvest Closes

BLUF

The Plymouth settlers and the Wampanoag remain at peace under a treaty Squanto helped negotiate, and a three-day harvest gathering with about 90 Wampanoag men closes today with no violence reported. The Wampanoag arrived armed after hearing gunfire; no source shows an invitation. One critical question remains: what were the odds that a group of English pilgrims would land in the one spot on the North American continent where an English speaking, Christianized indigenous person lived? Some observers believe it was God's providence, stating: "With God all things are possible."

Key Facts

  • Gathering. After the harvest, Plymouth’s governor, William Bradford, sends four men fowling, then hosts Massasoit (Ousamequin) and about 90 men for three days. The Wampanoag bring five deer to the governor and the captain (Winslow letter; Pilgrim Hall Museum).
  • Armed arrival. About 90 armed Wampanoag warriors arrive after hearing gunfire. A translator explains that the shots mark the harvest, and the Wampanoag stay several days, hunting deer, ducks, geese and fish (Native Partnership; History.com).
  • Treaty. Squanto helps negotiate a treaty that gives the Wampanoag an ally against the Narragansett, gives the English the dominant rule of law and restricts Wampanoag use and display of weapons at meetings (History.com; All That’s Interesting on the peace agreement).
  • Squanto’s service. Squanto teaches the settlers to plant corn and to fish, and he interprets and advocates for them with Massasoit. Governor Bradford calls him “a special instrument sent of God” (History.com; Beyond These Stone Walls).
  • Disease. A plague kills about two-thirds of roughly 70,000 people in 69 villages (History.com). Squanto returned in 1619 to find Patuxet empty, and Native Partnership says he is the only survivor of his village (All That’s Interesting; Native Partnership).
  • Settler numbers. About half of the 102 Mayflower passengers are alive: 24 men, five women and 24 children and teenagers (Plimoth Patuxet Museums; Beyond These Stone Walls).
  • Stored corn. During the first winter the settlers take corn from Wampanoag stores on Cape Cod. Native Partnership adds beans and says graves were robbed; Beyond These Stone Walls puts the take at ten bushels (Native Partnership; Beyond These Stone Walls).
  • Attendance. The colony’s attendees are 24 men, five women, 15 boys and nine girls. Plimoth Patuxet suggests others from Massasoit’s sphere also attend, possibly Wampanoag women and children who had been living nearby to farm (Plimoth Patuxet Museums).
  • Food position. Bradford records a small harvest, healthy settlers, cod and bass shared by family, fowl and wild turkeys as winter nears, and a ration of about a peck of meal per person each week, with Indian corn in the same amount since harvest (Pilgrim Hall Museum).
  • Primary record. Only two primary sources cover autumn 1621: Winslow’s letter and Bradford’s account. Winslow gives the counts and names fowl but no turkeys. Bradford gives no Wampanoag, day or deer counts and records wild turkeys in great numbers (Pilgrim Hall Museum; Plimoth Patuxet Museums).

Points of Dispute

Who rescued Squanto in Málaga. History.com reports historians say Catholic friars helped him escape to London (History.com). Native Partnership says he possibly escaped with friars’ help (Native Partnership). All That’s Interesting says monks rescued many captives and Squanto was apparently taken to a monastery (All That’s Interesting). Beyond These Stone Walls says two Spanish Jesuit priests intervened, sent him home as a free man and may have baptized him; its author cites no primary source for these points and argues that without the Pope and the Jesuits there would be no gathering (Beyond These Stone Walls). No primary source is available to settle the identity of the rescuers.

Whether the Wampanoag were invited. History.com says the settlers “likely” did not invite them (History.com). Native Partnership says they were not invited and came after hearing gunshots (Native Partnership). All That’s Interesting says it is unclear whether they came to give thanks (All That’s Interesting). Beyond These Stone Walls describes a feast with “their Native American neighbors” and does not address invitation (Beyond These Stone Walls). Plimoth Patuxet reads Winslow as describing English hosts who feast Massasoit’s men, and notes his letter does not say the Wampanoag were invited (Plimoth Patuxet Museums). The primary text identifies the hosts and is silent on invitations.

What was on the table. Native Partnership lists fish, waterfowl, venison, wild turkeys and corn across accounts and attributes the wild turkeys to Winslow’s publication, while its Wampanoag-side account names fowl without turkey (Native Partnership). Pilgrim Hall Museum reads Winslow’s passage as naming fowl and no turkeys, and Bradford’s as recording wild turkeys, venison, cod, bass and Indian corn without tying them to the gathering (Pilgrim Hall Museum). The primary passages settle that Winslow names no turkeys; they leave open whether turkey was served.

When and where the settlers landed. Native Partnership gives 9 Nov 1620, off Cape Cod, with Plymouth Rock first recorded in 1715 and first tied to a landing in 1741 (Native Partnership). Beyond These Stone Walls gives 11 Dec 1620, says the settlers’ journals describe the rock, and says it fell into obscurity until a 1741 wharf project (Beyond These Stone Walls). All That’s Interesting says only November 1620 (All That’s Interesting). The settlers’ own journals would settle the date; none was opened.

Scale of the epidemic. History.com counts about 70,000 people in 69 villages and does not name the disease (History.com). All That’s Interesting attributes the deaths to disease and names smallpox as a possibility (All That’s Interesting). Beyond These Stone Walls says a confederation of about 20,000 is down to about 1,000 (Beyond These Stone Walls). No source reconciles the populations.

Whether Squanto’s mediation protected or subordinated the Wampanoag. History.com says the treaty led to subjugation (History.com). Beyond These Stone Walls says his advocacy averted annihilation (Beyond These Stone Walls). Native Partnership says the English needed Wampanoag help and, though polite, held them in low regard (Native Partnership). All That’s Interesting reports Bradford’s view that Squanto pursued his own ends (All That’s Interesting). No source tests the treaty’s effect; the next season’s conduct will.

Who sent Squanto to Plymouth. History.com says he, an orphan, sought an alliance with Ousamequin and was well placed to help the arrivals (History.com). All That’s Interesting says Massasoit sent Samoset, who returned with several warriors and Squanto, and that Squanto’s better English made him the broker (All That’s Interesting). Beyond These Stone Walls says Massasoit, wary of settlers with guns and cannons, sent Squanto as an emissary and dates his entry to 22 Mar 1621 (Beyond These Stone Walls). No Wampanoag primary account of the decision is available.

How Squanto got home, and who held him. History.com says he left England on a ship for Captain John Mason, governor of the Newfoundland colony, and a second ship carried him south; it adds that a Wampanoag historian counts him as one of two people from the slave ship known to reach home (History.com). All That’s Interesting says Dermer hired him as interpreter for a Newfoundland expedition, hostile groups attacked, and Squanto stayed with Massasoit’s people, possibly as a prisoner (All That’s Interesting). Beyond These Stone Walls counts about three years in London and two in Newfoundland, then a voyage with Dermer, and says Massasoit captured both men (Beyond These Stone Walls). The sources agree on a 1619 return and differ on the route and on Massasoit’s role.

Whether the Cape Cod corn was repaid. Native Partnership says the settlers robbed Wampanoag graves and took corn and beans, and it does not mention repayment (Native Partnership). Beyond These Stone Walls says the settlers, unable to plant, took ten bushels from a storage site, planted the seed corn, and that Squanto negotiated a fair trade to repay the theft (Beyond These Stone Walls). The author cites no primary source for the repayment.

Background

The Wampanoag, whose name means people of the first light, have lived at Patuxet for at least 10,000 years, and Squanto is born there around 1580 (History.com; All That’s Interesting). 1537: Pope Paul III issues the bull Sublimis Deus, which bars Catholic governments from enslaving American Indians (Beyond These Stone Walls). 1614: Thomas Hunt, left in command by Captain John Smith, lures about two dozen Patuxet villagers, Squanto among them, onto his ship and sails for Málaga to sell them (Beyond These Stone Walls; All That’s Interesting). Spanish churchmen help Squanto leave Spain, and the sources name them differently. He reaches London and learns English in the household of John Slaney, a Newfoundland merchant official (History.com). 1619: Captain Thomas Dermer carries him home from Newfoundland. Patuxet is empty, Dermer is attacked and later dies of his wounds, and Squanto stays with Massasoit’s people, possibly as a prisoner (All That’s Interesting). 1620: The settlers sail on 16 Sep after the Speedwell springs leaks and its passengers transfer to the Mayflower; the author of Beyond These Stone Walls says the leaks were deliberate and cites no primary source (Beyond These Stone Walls). The Mayflower, bound for a point south of the Hudson River, puts ashore off Cape Cod, where the settlers take stored corn (Native Partnership). The settlers winter at Patuxet, now Plymouth, and about half die (Beyond These Stone Walls). Massasoit weighs whether to befriend or expel them and sends Samoset, an Abenaki man, who returns with Squanto (All That’s Interesting). Beyond These Stone Walls dates Squanto’s arrival to 22 Mar 1621 and says he speaks near-perfect English. Through the spring and summer he teaches planting and fishing, and a peace agreement follows (History.com; All That’s Interesting).

  3. Whether Squanto remains alive and in Plymouth’s service on 31 Dec 1622. Assessment: Likely (moderate confidence), based on no source reporting illness and his continuing value to the governor.
  4. Whether the colony’s winter food supply holds through 31 Mar 1622. Assessment: Likely (low confidence), based on Bradford’s record of fish, fowl, wild turkeys and weekly meal rations, weighed against the small harvest.
